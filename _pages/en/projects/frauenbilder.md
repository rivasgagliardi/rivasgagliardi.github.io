**University of Cologne, since 2024**

How does a woman come to speak in the early modern period, and what kind of knowledge arises where she takes the floor? The project pursues this question in the framed novella collection: in works that stage storytelling itself as sociable speech, with rules for who narrates, who listens and who judges. At its centre stand Marguerite de Navarre’s *Heptaméron* (1559) and the two collections of María de Zayas: the *Novelas amorosas y ejemplares* (1637) and the *Desengaños amorosos* (1647). Marguerite de Navarre (1492–1549), queen of Navarre and sister of Francis I of France, left the *Heptaméron* unfinished, and it appeared posthumously. Of María de Zayas, a Madrilenian of the Siglo de Oro, almost nothing is known beyond her books, which were among the most widely read of their century. The term *licentia*, which gives the project its title, meant two things in the period: a freedom of speech and a permission that had to be obtained.

Three approaches interlock in my analysis: a historical semantics of *licentia*, reaching from the rhetorical tradition to the dictionaries of the age, a narratology of the frame, asking who listens and who speaks in these texts, and a reading of the minor female characters, in whom the reach and the limit of this staged women’s knowledge can be read.
